How routing works

You place points, and the planner connects each pair of neighbouring points with its own leg. Legs are calculated by BRouter running on our server. Roads, trails, surfaces and cycle routes come from OpenStreetMap, elevation from a built-in terrain model.

The router does not look for the shortest path but for the cheapest one. Every kind of road has a price per kilometre, different in each profile. For gravel, a good dirt road costs 1.1, a quiet paved road 1.3, a secondary road 2.2 and a main road about 8.6. So gravel takes a dirt road when the detour is short, rides quiet tarmac without fuss and stays off busy roads. For MTB the gap between dirt and tarmac is much bigger, so it is drawn to dirt and trails much more. Climbs, turns and access rules add to the price: the router never sends you where bikes are not allowed.

A leg by road can be at most 100 km in a straight line between its two points; for longer distances add points in between. Straight legs have no limit.

When you drag a point, only the two legs next to it are recalculated; the rest comes from memory, so editing a long route stays fast.

Settings

Surface. “More asphalt” makes unpaved ways 2.5 times more expensive, “More dirt” does the same to tarmac. It is a preference, not a ban: the other surface is still used where it saves a long detour. When OpenStreetMap has no surface tag, ordinary streets and roads count as paved, tracks and trails as unpaved.

Climbs. “Fewer” adds a price to every metre of climbing steeper than 1.5%, and the router starts going around hills. “Don’t avoid” removes that price. The router cannot look for “more climbs”: it can only penalise, never reward.

Follow cycleways. Cycleways, cycle lanes and signed cycle routes (local, regional, national, EuroVelo) keep their price, everything else costs 1.6 times more. The route may get a little longer but follows cycle infrastructure. Not available for hiking.

Max grade N%. Climbs steeper than the limit become very expensive, and the router looks for a way around. It is a soft limit: when there is no way around, the steep part stays, and the elevation profile marks it in red along the top with its total length.

Straight legs

If the trail you need is missing from OpenStreetMap, switch the leg to a straight line: right-click the line, long-press on a phone, or press B over it; Shift+click on the map adds one straight leg, and B with the cursor away from the line draws all new legs straight. A straight leg is drawn as a grey dashed line; its elevation comes from an open terrain model.

Riding time

A rough estimate, hence the “~”. For bikes it is the profile’s average speed adjusted for climbing. For hiking it is the walking time used on trail signs in the Alps (DIN 33466): 4 km/h on the flat, 300 m up or 500 m down per hour, without breaks. Your fitness, load and weather change it, and the planner knows none of them.

Mountain passes

The “Passes” layer combines two sources:

  • OpenStreetMap: about 37,000 passes worldwide, many with elevation, some with a grade.
  • FSTR catalogue: the open pass classifier of the Russian Sport Tourism Federation, about 7,500 passes in the former USSR and neighbouring ranges. It has grades, terrain, what the pass connects and first crossings. Names and descriptions from it are in Russian.

When a pass is in both sources and the points are close, they are merged into one record, and the grade is taken from the FSTR catalogue. The pass card always tells where the data comes from.

Zoomed out, only graded passes are shown; closer in, named ones appear, and from zoom 12 all of them.

Grades

GradeWhat to expect
NCNon-graded. A trail or a gentle grassy slope, no snow or ice.

1BModerate. Steep scree and snow, short rock sections. Ice axe self-belay, a rope in places.
2AIntermediate. Snow, ice and rock, covered glaciers. Crampons, ropes, roped travel.
2BDifficult. Steep ice and rock, long rope work. Mountaineering skills required.
3AVery difficult. High altitude, steep ice and rock walls.
3BThe hardest passes: technical climbing, high altitude, rockfall and avalanche danger.

An asterisk after a grade (for example 1B*) marks a pass at the upper end of its grade.

A grade describes typical summer conditions. In early spring, late autumn or a snowy year a pass can be harder, and glaciers have retreated a lot over the last decades. Read recent trip reports before you go, for example on westra.ru and tssr.ru (in Russian).

Limitations

  • The map is as good as OpenStreetMap is complete. The router learns about a new trail or a closed road only after the next data update.
  • Elevation comes from a terrain model with about 30 m resolution. Short steep pitches can be smoothed, and total climbing may differ from your watch by 5–10%.
  • The grade limit is soft: it steers around steep parts when it can, but does not forbid them.
  • Riding time and surfaces without an OpenStreetMap tag are estimates.

Send to Garmin (beta)

Garmin offers no public interface for other apps to put courses on your watch. No Mapa therefore uses the same sign-in as the Garmin Connect app, through the open-source library python-garminconnect.

  • Connecting. Your Garmin email and password go to our server over HTTPS. The server signs in to Garmin with them once; if Garmin asks for a code from your two-step verification, you enter it in the same window. The password is then dropped and never written anywhere.
  • What is kept. Garmin returns an access token, like the one your phone keeps after you sign in. The server stores it encrypted with a key that lives only on the server, and Garmin refreshes it from time to time.
  • What it allows. A token is not limited to courses: whoever holds it can do in your Garmin Connect account what you can. No Mapa uses it only to upload a course and to queue it for your chosen watch.
  • Ending it. “Disconnect Garmin” in the send window deletes the token from our server. Changing your Garmin password also ends it. Deleting your No Mapa account deletes it too.
  • Risks. This is unofficial: Garmin may change its sign-in and break it without warning, and its terms do not cover this kind of access. If you are not comfortable with that, use “Download GPX” and import the file in Garmin Connect yourself.

Account and data

The map, routing and links work without an account. Your routes are then kept only in this browser. When you share a route or sign in with a route open, a copy of that route is stored on our server under a random short link, so the link opens it; without an account it is not tied to you, up to 25 links a day from one address.

Signing in with Google or with an alexe.pt account lets you keep routes on our server and open them on any device. From Google we receive only your name, email address and a stable account identifier; we do not get your contacts, files or anything else.

What we store: your name and email; the routes, tracks and checkpoints you save; short links you create; if you connect Garmin, an encrypted access token for it (your Garmin password is used once to connect and is not stored); a session cookie that keeps you signed in for 30 days. We do not sell or share this data and do not use it for advertising. Apart from Google Analytics described below, there are no third-party trackers in the planner.

Limits: up to 1,000 routes and tracks or 25 MB per account, 50 short links and 15 Garmin sends a day.

Deleting: “Delete account” in the account menu removes your account and everything stored for it at once. Short links you shared stop working. For questions write to nomapa@alexe.pt.

Where: the server is in the European Union (OVH).

Who runs it: Alexe Politov, a private person, for himself and friends. The service is free, carries no advertising and earns nothing. Your data is processed to provide what you ask for (keeping and syncing your routes), and for no other purpose.

Analytics. To see which features are used and where the planner breaks, it counts actions with Google Analytics: for example that a route was built, a GPX downloaded or a setting changed, with the route type and the length rounded to 5 km. It never sends coordinates, the map position, route or place names, your name or email; the page address is sent without the part after #, which holds the map position. On your first visit the planner asks whether Google Analytics may set cookies. Until you allow it, and after “No cookies”, it runs in Google’s consent mode without cookies and sends only anonymous counts. Google signals and advertising features are off. You can change your choice any time under “Cookie settings” in the account menu. Google’s privacy policy applies to what Google receives.

Cookies and browser storage.

  • nc_session: keeps you signed in, set only when you sign in, 30 days. Removed when you sign out.
  • nc_oauth: protects the Google sign-in against forgery, lives 10 minutes during sign-in.
  • _ga, _ga_HDTV62D1ZM: Google Analytics, set only if you allow analytics cookies; they tell visits of the same browser apart, up to 2 years.
  • Browser storage (IndexedDB, localStorage, sessionStorage) on your device: your routes and tracks, the last map view, chosen layers and settings, the route open in the tab, your cookie choice, and lists of photos found along routes (links only, kept up to 30 days). It never leaves the browser unless you sign in and sync.

Other services your browser contacts: map tiles and the heatmaps come from their providers (OpenStreetMap and CyclOSM, OpenTopoMap, mapy.com, Google, Esri, Microsoft Bing, Yandex, Waymarked Trails, Ride with GPS, AWS terrain tiles). With points along the route switched on, it also loads OpenFreeMap tiles for the route corridor, asks Wikimedia Commons for photos near the route; springs (from OpenStreetMap via Overpass) and street photos from Mapillary and Panoramax come through our server. Like any web request, they see your IP address and which map area is loaded; their own privacy policies apply. Google sees your sign-in only if you choose to sign in with Google; Garmin only if you connect it.

How long: routes and the account stay until you delete them. Server logs with IP addresses are kept for security and overwritten as they fill up (at most 30 MB per service), usually within days to a few weeks. Cached routing answers hold no personal data.

Your rights: under the GDPR you can get a copy of your data, correct or delete it, and object to its processing. “Delete account” removes everything at once; for anything else write to nomapa@alexe.pt. You can also complain to the Portuguese data protection authority (CNPD).
