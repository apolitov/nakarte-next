How routing works

You place points, and the planner connects each pair of neighbouring points with its own leg. Legs are calculated by BRouter running on our server. Roads, trails, surfaces and cycle routes come from OpenStreetMap, elevation from a built-in terrain model.

The router does not look for the shortest path but for the cheapest one. Every kind of road has a price per kilometre, different in each profile. For gravel, a good dirt road costs 1.1, a quiet paved road 1.3, a secondary road 2.2 and a main road about 8.6. So gravel takes a dirt road when the detour is short, rides quiet tarmac without fuss and stays off busy roads. For MTB the gap between dirt and tarmac is much bigger, so it is drawn to dirt and trails much more. Climbs, turns and access rules add to the price: the router never sends you where bikes are not allowed.

When you drag a point, only the two legs next to it are recalculated; the rest comes from memory, so editing a long route stays fast.

Settings

Surface. “More asphalt” makes unpaved ways 2.5 times more expensive, “More dirt” does the same to tarmac. It is a preference, not a ban: the other surface is still used where it saves a long detour. When OpenStreetMap has no surface tag, ordinary streets and roads count as paved, tracks and trails as unpaved.

Climbs. “Fewer” adds a price to every metre of climbing steeper than 1.5%, and the router starts going around hills. “Don’t avoid” removes that price. The router cannot look for “more climbs”: it can only penalise, never reward.

Follow cycleways. Cycleways, cycle lanes and signed cycle routes (local, regional, national, EuroVelo) keep their price, everything else costs 1.6 times more. The route may get a little longer but follows cycle infrastructure. Not available for hiking.

Max grade N%. Climbs steeper than the limit become very expensive, and the router looks for a way around. It is a soft limit: when there is no way around, the steep part stays, and the elevation profile marks it in red along the top with its total length.

Straight legs

If the trail you need is missing from OpenStreetMap, switch the leg to a straight line: right-click the line, long-press on a phone, or press ⌘I / Ctrl+I. A straight leg is drawn as a grey dashed line; its elevation comes from an open terrain model.

Riding time

A rough estimate from the profile’s average speed adjusted for climbing, hence the “~”. Your fitness, load and weather change it, and the planner knows none of them.

Surface comes from OpenStreetMap tags. Without a tag it is guessed from the road type, and the profile marks such parts as “no data”.

Mountain passes

The “Passes” layer combines two sources:

  • OpenStreetMap: about 37,000 passes worldwide, many with elevation, some with a grade.
  • FSTR catalogue: the open pass classifier of the Russian Sport Tourism Federation, about 7,500 passes in the former USSR and neighbouring ranges. It has grades, terrain, what the pass connects and first crossings. Names and descriptions from it are in Russian.

When a pass is in both sources and the points are close, they are merged into one record, and the grade is taken from the FSTR catalogue. The pass card always tells where the data comes from.

Zoomed out, only graded passes are shown; closer in, named ones appear, and from zoom 12 all of them.

GradeWhat to expect

1BModerate. Steep scree and snow, short rock sections. Ice axe self-belay, a rope in places.
2AIntermediate. Snow, ice and rock, covered glaciers. Crampons, ropes, roped travel.
2BDifficult. Steep ice and rock, long rope work. Mountaineering skills required.
3AVery difficult. High altitude, steep ice and rock walls.
3BThe hardest passes: technical climbing, high altitude, rockfall and avalanche danger.

An asterisk after a grade (for example 1B*) marks a pass at the upper end of its grade.

A grade describes typical summer conditions. In early spring, late autumn or a snowy year a pass can be harder, and glaciers have retreated a lot over the last decades. Read recent trip reports before you go, for example on westra.ru and tssr.ru (in Russian).

Limitations

  • The map is as good as OpenStreetMap is complete. The router learns about a new trail or a closed road only after the next data update.
  • Elevation comes from a terrain model with about 30 m resolution. Short steep pitches can be smoothed, and total climbing may differ from your watch by 5–10%.
  • The grade limit is soft: it steers around steep parts when it can, but does not forbid them.
  • Riding time and surfaces without an OpenStreetMap tag are estimates.
